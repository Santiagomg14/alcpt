16.(Audio item, no printed stem.)

  • rare roast beef
  • apple pie
  • cold meats

ExplanationA food-vocabulary item. Note that "rare," when applied to meat, means lightly cooked and still red inside, not "unusual."

19.The bank is opposite the post office.

  • The bank is on a different street.
  • The bank is across the street from the post office.
  • The bank is just behind the post office.
  • The bank is next to the post office.

ExplanationThe preposition "opposite" is used to describe a position on the other side of a particular area, such as a road or a room, facing someone or something else. In urban navigation and giving directions, saying one building is opposite another means they are facing each other, typically separated by a street. "On a different street" is incorrect because "opposite" implies a specific spatial relationship along the same street.

28.The box is light.

  • The box is not heavy.
  • The box is not hard.
  • The light is on.
  • It has a handle.

ExplanationIn the context of this sentence, the word "light" is an adjective describing weight. It means that the object has little weight and is easy to move or lift. Therefore, saying the box is "not heavy" is the correct paraphrase. "It has a handle" is incorrect because, while a handle might make a box easier to lift, the sentence says nothing about a handle.

30.Are they alike?

  • Yes, they look the same.
  • Yes, I like them.
  • Yes, they will go on a trip.

ExplanationThe adjective "alike" means having a resemblance or being similar to each other. When asking if two people are alike, the speaker is inquiring whether they share similar physical traits or characteristics. "Yes, I like them" is incorrect because it uses the verb "like" (to enjoy or appreciate), which is a different word entirely.

32.Where did you put my pencil?

  • I laid it on the bookcase.
  • I bought it at the bookstore.
  • I got it from Miss Bell.

ExplanationThe question asks where you put the pencil, seeking the specific location where an object was placed. The verb "laid" is the past tense of "lay," which means to put or place something down horizontally. "On the bookcase" correctly answers the "where" part of the question. "I got it from Miss Bell" is incorrect because it describes the source or origin of the item (from whom) rather than where it was placed.

34.He is going to take up medicine this year. What is he going to do?

  • He's going to study medicine.
  • He's going to continue working as a doctor.

ExplanationThe phrasal verb "take up" means to begin a new activity, hobby, or profession. When someone says they are going to take up medicine, it indicates they are starting their path in the medical field, which involves beginning a course of study to become a doctor. "Continue working as a doctor" is incorrect because "take up" implies a new start, whereas continuing suggests he was already doing it.

38.Twenty-five students in all made a trip to the zoo.

  • Altogether, there were 25 students on the trip.
  • There were 25 trips made to the zoo.
  • The students all were 25 years old.
  • There were 25 students who called the zoo.

ExplanationThe phrase "in all" is an idiomatic expression used to indicate a total count or the entirety of a group. In the original sentence, "25 students in all" means that the total number of students who visited the zoo was 25. The phrase "altogether" is a synonym for "in all," and "on the trip" accurately reflects the action of making a trip to the zoo.

39.James is an efficient worker.

  • He does his work quickly and well.
  • He supervises other workers.
  • He complains a lot.

ExplanationThe term "efficient" describes a person who works in a way that produces the desired results without wasting time, effort, or resources. Doing work quickly and well is a direct definition of efficiency. In military and professional contexts, efficiency is a key performance trait denoting high productivity and competence. Supervising others is a different role and is not implied by the word.

40.The cadet was not guilty of the charge.

  • The cadet was found innocent.
  • The cadet was given a fine.
  • The cadet was sentenced to prison.
  • The cadet was found guilty.

ExplanationIn a legal or military justice context, the phrase "not guilty" is a verdict indicating that the person accused (the cadet) did not commit the offense. Being found innocent is the direct synonym for being not guilty of a specific charge. "The cadet was given a fine" is incorrect because a fine is a form of punishment, which would only occur if the cadet were found guilty.

41.Bob Moore wanted to inspect the new oxygen mask.

  • He wanted to leave it.
  • He wanted to look at it.
  • He wanted to get one.
  • He wanted to buy it.

ExplanationTo "inspect" means to examine something carefully and closely, in other words, to look at it. Wanting to buy, obtain, or leave the mask are different actions not implied by the verb "inspect."

42.Mike spread out the maps.

  • He opened the maps.
  • He folded the maps.
  • He marked the maps.
  • He copied the maps.

ExplanationThe phrasal verb "spread out" means to extend, unfold, or open something so that it covers a larger area, often to make it easier to see or use. In military operations, spreading out maps is a standard procedure for mission planning, navigation, and briefings, to allow personnel to view the entire terrain. "He folded the maps" is incorrect because folding is the opposite action; it involves making the maps smaller for storage or transport.

43.The air is dense at sea level.

  • The air is thick.
  • The air is thin.
  • The air is hot.
  • The air is cool.

ExplanationIn the context of meteorology and aviation, "dense" refers to the high concentration of molecules in the air. This is synonymous with saying the air is thick. At sea level, atmospheric pressure is at its highest, meaning the air is at its most dense. As altitude increases, the air becomes less dense, or thin. "The air is thin" is incorrect because thin is the antonym of dense.

45.Mr. Long liked the cartoons in the evening newspaper. What were they?

  • headlines
  • drawings
  • stories
  • news

ExplanationIn a newspaper, cartoons are drawings or comic panels, often humorous or satirical. Headlines are the titles of articles, while stories and news refer to written reporting, none of which are drawings.

46.And it said she got a new filling yesterday. What did she get?

  • a cavity filled by the dentist
  • her car filled with gasoline

ExplanationIn a medical or dental context, a "filling" refers to the material (such as composite or amalgam) used to fill a hole or cavity in a tooth caused by decay. This is a common term used in general English and often tested in military language proficiency exams to ensure personnel understand health-related terminology. "Her car filled with gasoline" is incorrect because, while "fill" is used for fuel tanks, the noun "a filling" refers specifically to dentistry.

47.How did the hole get there?

  • Edward dug it.
